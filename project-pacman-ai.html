Pac-Man AI

Make Pac-Man play well, seeing the world only through a restricted API — and, in the hardest version, seeing almost none of it. Coursework during my BSc.

  • Python
  • Markov decision process
  • Value iteration

The setup

We were handed a Pac-Man clone in Python and a limited set of API calls for asking about the world. Everything the agent knew had to come through those calls, so the work was in deciding what to do with the information rather than in reading the game state directly.

The coursework came in versions of increasing difficulty. The easier ones granted full visibility — the whole map, every ghost, every piece of food. The hardest took nearly all of that away.

The hard version

Two things made the final challenge genuinely difficult, and they compound each other.

Pac-Man could only see a few squares around himself. No map, no distant ghosts, no knowing where the remaining food was. Decisions had to be made from a small moving window of the world.

Movement was not deterministic. Choosing a direction gave an 80% chance of going that way, and 10% each of veering left or right instead. That sounds survivable until you are running from a ghost down a corridor: a bad roll turns you into a wall, you do not move at all, and the ghost closes the gap. Losing a life to a coin-flip you never chose is the defining hazard of that version.

Modelling it

I treated the world as a Markov decision process and solved it with value iteration: assign a reward to each square, then repeatedly propagate values outward until they settle, so every square carries not just what is on it but how good it is to be near what is around it. Pac-Man then walks uphill.

Because the transition model is the 80/10/10 split rather than certainty, the values already account for the possibility of ending up somewhere you did not choose.

The reward function had a state to it:

  • Ghosts were terminal states with a large negative reward — reaching one ends the run.
  • Food carried a small positive reward.
  • Capsules carried a larger one, being worth going out of the way for.
  • While the capsule was active and ghosts were edible, those values flipped: capsules dropped low, so a second one would not be wasted during the window, and ghosts became strongly positive — worth chasing rather than fleeing.

Giving ghosts a bigger footprint

The part I was most pleased with. Penalising only the square a ghost occupies quietly assumes you can always step away from it — and with non-deterministic movement, you cannot. Standing next to a ghost to grab one last piece of food is a bet, and roughly one time in five the move you chose is not the move you make.

So I widened the danger. The squares around a ghost were treated as terminal too, giving each ghost a bubble several squares across and making it behave, as far as the algorithm was concerned, like a much larger ghost.

The effect was that Pac-Man stopped taking marginal trades. Food and capsules sitting right beside a ghost were no longer worth it, because the value function now priced in the chance of a move going wrong at exactly the moment it mattered most. It made the agent noticeably more cautious, and noticeably more likely to finish the level.
